Features of militarism in Japan

  • Military dictatorship: the militarists controlled the cabinet. Under military dictatorship, all opposition to its rule was silenced by force.
  • Control over thoughts and emperor worship: to unite the Japanese people, the military government promoted emperor-centred nationalism. The military controls over thought and ideas. School textbooks were used to promote war and emperor-centred nationalism.
  • Anti-communism: Japanese militarists believed that the idea of class struggle would weaken unity among the people and even destroy national tradition.
  • Military and territorial expansion: Japan's expansion aimed to get land for its surplus population and resources for its industries. It expanded in China and Asia step by step.
  • State control: the military government controlled all resources and economic activities in the state. The whole nation was military-oriented to continue the war effort.

3. The Failure of Collective Security (The League of Nations)

After WWI, the League of Nations was created to maintain world peace through "collective security" — the idea that countries would act together to stop any aggressor. It had 58 member states at its peak. Its member countries would collectively mediate, impose sanctions or take military action to stop aggression.

In the 1920s, the League of Nations settled some disputes among the smaller countries, but it failed to stop the invasions by Japan, Germany, and Italy. They ignored the criticism of the League and even withdrew from it one after another. The League was so weak and ineffective because:

  • Lack of representation: Its operation mainly relied on the support of Britain and France. Other powers joined only briefly or never joined (e.g. the US), so it failed to establish its authority.
  • Inefficient mechanism: Resolutions had to be passed unanimously by all members of the League. This made it difficult for the League to carry out actions.
  • Weak military power: The League did not have a standing army. The League could only condemn or impose economic sanctions on the aggressors.

Examples of the failure of the League in the 1930s

  • Japan: In 1931, Japan invaded Manchuria (in northeast China). The League condemned Japan and urged it to withdraw, but Japan ignored the demand and simply left the League in 1933. The League did nothing to stop them.
  • Germany: In 1932, the League convened the Geneva Disarmament Conference. When Hitler rose to power in 1933, Germany left both the disarmament conference and the League. The League also did nothing to stop the German aggression in the 1930s.
  • Italy: In 1935, Italy invaded Abyssinia (nowadays Ethiopia). The League tried to use economic sanctions to stop the Italian invasion, but they were weak and ineffective. In 1936, Italy occupied Abyssinia, and it left the League the next year.
  • The Result: Dictators realized the League was powerless. They learned they could act aggressively without facing any real consequences.

4. The Policy of Appeasement

The appeasement policy was the foreign policy adopted by Britain and France in the 1930s when facing the expansion of the Axis Powers. It meant giving in to the demands of the invaders in order to avoid war.

  • Why Appease? Britain and France were still traumatized by the horrors of WWI. They were also struggling with the Great Depression and were not ready to fight another war. Many also felt the Treaty of Versailles had been too harsh on Germany.
  • The Munich Agreement (1938): The most famous example of the appeasement policy was the Munich Agreement. In September 1938, Hitler demanded the Sudetenland (a region of Czechoslovakia). Britain and France did not stop it and even signed the agreement with Hitler. They forced Czechoslovakia to cede the Sudetenland to Germany in return for Hitler's promise to respect the new Czech borders.
  • The Consequence: Appeasement completely failed. It did not satisfy Hitler, but it fuelled German ambitions and convinced him that Britain and France were too weak to fight. When Hitler invaded Poland in September 1939, appeasement ended, and WWII began.

5. Isolationism of the United States

  • After the Paris Peace Conference, the United States maintained its policy of isolationism and insisted on not interfering in European affairs. The US did not join the League of Nations after WWI.
  • In the 1930s, the US Congress passed the Neutrality Acts, which banned the export of military equipment to any country at war.
  • The United States only condemned the aggressions of Italy and Germany without taking any strong action. → encouraged the aggressions of the totalitarian states.

5. The Soviet Union drawn closer to Germany

  • German expansions to the East made the Soviet Union feel threatened.
  • The Soviet Union intended to unite with Britain and France to fight against Germany. However, the Soviet Union was not trusted by the Western countries.
    Eg. Stalin was not invited to the Munich Conference in 1938.
  • Stalin doubted that Britain and France planned to play Germany against the Soviet Union. → The Soviet Union changed to improve relations with Germany.
  • In August 1939, the Nazi-Soviet Non-Aggression Pact was signed, which stated that they would not wage war against each other for ten years.
  • Germany and the Soviet Union also agreed secretly to partition Poland and to divide their spheres of influence in Eastern Europe.

Events and immediate cause leading to the Second World War

  • After Germany annexed the rest of Czechoslovakia, Britain and France abandoned their appeasement policy. They were determined to safeguard the independence of Poland and other countries.

The immediate cause: German invasion of Poland (September, 1939)

  • Hitler believed that Britain and France would not go to war for Poland.
  • The signing of the Nazi-Soviet Non-Aggression Pact in August 1939 encouraged Germany to invade Poland.
→ Britain and France gave up the appeasement policy and declared war on Germany → The Second World War began

2. Major developments in the Second World War

1939

War broke out

In September, Germany invaded Poland. The Second World War broke out. Poland fell within a month.

1940

Western and Northern Europe fell

From April to June 1940, the Germans conquered Denmark, Norway, Belgium, the Netherlands, and France by blitzkrieg.

350,000 Allied soldiers were evacuated from Continental Europe to Britain to save their strength. Italy also declared war on the Allies at that time.

Battle of Britain

From July to December 1940, the Germans continued to bomb Britain, causing heavy casualties but failing to force Britain to surrender.

Extension of the war

In 1940 – 1941, the war extended to North Africa, the Middle East and Southern Europe. Germany controlled almost all of Europe.

1941

Germany invaded the Soviet Union

In June 1941, Germany invaded the Soviet Union but failed to force it to surrender. The Soviet Union won the battle of Moscow, stopping Germany's rapid expansion on the European continent.

Attack on Pearl Harbor

On 7 December 1941, Japan attacked the US naval base at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii. The US thus declared war on the Axis Powers. The Pacific War broke out.

1942

Pacific War

By May 1942, Japan occupied Hong Kong and most of Southeast Asia, including Malaya and Singapore, and established the "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere".

Reversed the situation

In June 1942, the US defeated the Japanese at the Battle of Midway, marking the end of Japanese superiority.

In the same year, Germany suffered a severe defeat by the Soviet Red Army in the Battle of Stalingrad. The Red Army launched a counter-attack.

1943

Italy surrendered

In September 1943, the Allies invaded Italy. Italy then surrendered. Mussolini was rescued by Germany. He was executed by Italians in April 1945.

1944

Restoration of France

In June 1944, the Allies landed on the Normandy beaches in France and then marched to Paris in August to liberate France.

1945

Germany surrendered

At the end of April 1945, the Allies captured Berlin, the capital of Germany. On 30 April, Hitler committed suicide. On 7 May, Germany surrendered unconditionally.

Japan surrendered

In August 1945, the US dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in Japan. Japan declared unconditional surrender. The Second World War ended.

3. Results of WWII

  • The victorious countries of WWII did not hold a peace conference and sign a peace treaty in a united manner as they did after WWI.
  • Instead, post-WWII settlements were mainly reached during several wartime conferences among the US, Britain, and the USSR, such as the Yalta Conference in February 1945 and the Potsdam Conference from July to August 1945.
  • After WWII, the world was immediately split into the capitalist and communist blocs.
  • Treaties with the defeated countries were signed several years later.

  • A treaty was drafted in Paris in July 1946 and formally signed in 1947.
  • All Fascist organisations would be broken up in the defeated countries.
  • According to the Potsdam Conference, Germany and Berlin were split into four occupation zones controlled by the US, Britain, France and the Soviet Union.
  • Japan was occupied by the US after WWII.

4. Impacts of the two world wars

(a) Casualties and economic losses

  • The two wars resulted in heavy casualties: the First World War killed about 8 million people and injured more than 30 million; the Second World War killed about 75 million and injured tens of millions. Some of them were victims of massacres. The Nazi genocide of the Jews killed about 6 million Jews.
  • The wars destroyed many cities and production facilities. Problems like shortages of food and goods, inflation, and unemployment emerged, causing serious damage to the economies of various countries.

(b) Formation of peacekeeping organizations

  • The devastation of war made nations realize the importance of maintaining world peace. The founding of the League of Nations after the First World War was an important step forward in international cooperation.
  • In October 1945, the United Nations (UN) was founded and replaced the League of Nations. The United Nations has drawn lessons from the League and continued to promote global cooperation.

(c) Establishment of a new international order

  • After WWII, colonies in Asia and Africa under European powers became independent. The European countries suffered a serious decline in economic and military power and lost their leading position in the world.
  • At this time, the US and the Soviet Union emerged as the superpowers of the post-war world. Their political and economic power far exceeded that of other countries. However, the two countries had already revealed their differences and mutual distrust at the wartime conferences. This caused the "Cold War" after WWII.

(d) Improvement of the status of women

  • As large numbers of men were conscripted into the military during the war, many women went out to work so as to keep industry and society running. They even joined the armed forces and fought on the front lines.
  • The social status of women was raised after the war. They received more job opportunities, freedoms, and rights (e.g. the right to vote).

5. Wars and inventions

5.1 Use of new armaments

  • The First World War introduced new weapons such as tanks, submarines, and fighter planes. The mode of warfare changed from a surface war limited to sea and land to a three-dimensional war of sea, land, and air.
  • World War II continued the scale of World War I. New types of armaments, such as aircraft carriers, nuclear weapons, bombers, and rockets, became more dangerous and caused greater destruction.
  • The atomic bomb was the most deadly weapon of World War II and brought about the Nuclear Age.
  • After the war, the US and the Soviet Union competed to develop more powerful nuclear weapons (e.g. hydrogen bombs), threatening world peace and security. The question of how to limit nuclear weapons remains an important international issue.

5.2 Medicine

  • Many soldiers died of wound infections. New medical products were developed to prevent this. Examples include Dakin's solution for cleaning wounds, Thomas splints for broken bones, and penicillin (an antibiotic) for bacterial infections.
  • In addition, plastic surgery and blood transfusion techniques were introduced and saved many lives. They are still commonly used today.

5.3 Daily necessities

During the war, countries improved their uniforms to meet the needs of their soldiers:

  • For example, the US Air Force wore warmer zip-up jackets, while European officers wore light, waterproof trench coats. Zip-up jackets and trench coats have become increasingly popular as daily accessories and clothing today.

The war also encouraged the development of new materials:

  • Stainless steel, invented by British scientists, was hard and durable. It was used in the manufacture of weapons and aircraft engines during World War I and for making cutlery after the war.
  • Cellucotton, which is five times more absorbent than cotton, was a common medical product during the war. It was made into tissues and sanitary napkins.

5.4 Computer

During the Second World War, countries developed calculating devices. For example:

  • Britain invented the "Colossus computer" in 1943 to calculate and crack the codes of the German army.
  • The world's first computer, "ENIAC", was made in the US in 1946, which laid the foundation for the development of modern computers.

5.5 Radar

  • With the widespread use of fighter planes and submarines, radar systems were developed to detect the location of the enemy.
  • After the war, radar was used for a variety of functions, such as monitoring the weather, the environment, and traffic conditions.
  • The microwave principle of radar brought about the invention of microwave ovens.

5.6 Jet engines

  • In 1939, Germany developed a jet engine for fighter planes.
  • This engine provided more driving force and allowed the aircraft to fly faster.
  • After the war, civilian aircraft also gradually adopted jet engines, thus promoting the development of long-distance transport.

5.7 Nuclear power

  • During the Second World War, the US actively researched nuclear energy and developed nuclear weapons, hence the "Nuclear Age".
  • After the war, countries developed nuclear energy for military and civilian purposes.
  • In the 1950s, the US and the Soviet Union mastered the technology of nuclear power generation and built nuclear power plants.
  • In the second half of the 20th century, nuclear energy became increasingly popular as countries developed energy alternatives to fossil fuels.
  • By the early 2020s, more than 30 countries used nuclear power to generate electricity, accounting for about 10% of global electricity.
